Japanese vs English

Andrew Brittle, 2026
This follows on from a note on how the Minna no Nihongo motion-sentence frame generalises beyond travel sentences — Japanese sentence structure →. That note kept running into the same question: why does one particle keep changing job depending on the verb? These are the bigger structural differences behind that.

These aren't really about vocabulary, or even grammar rules in the usual sense — they're about what each language is organised around.

English is subject-prominent; Japanese is topic-prominent

This is an actual classification from linguists Charles Li and Sandra Thompson. English sentences are built around a grammatical subject, fused to the verb through agreement and word order. Japanese sentences are built around a topic — what's being discussed — with everything after it as commentary on that topic. The topic doesn't have to be the grammatical subject at all.

That's the real substance behind は vs が: が marks new information being introduced or asked about, while は marks something already established as the topic of conversation.

だれが行きますか。
"Who is going?" (が — asking, nothing established yet)

Once that distinction is internalised, sentences that drop the subject entirely stop feeling like something is missing — Japanese was never built around having one in the first place.

Particles mark grammatical role, not position

English tells you what's the subject and what's the object almost entirely through word order ("the dog bit the man" vs "the man bit the dog" — same words, opposite meaning). Japanese uses particles — は/が/を/に/で/と — attached to each phrase to do that job instead, which frees up the order underneath them.

That's the actual mechanical reason the six slots in the sentence-structure frame can be rearranged and still stay grammatical, as long as the verb stays at the end: each phrase carries its own tag, so the order works more like labelled luggage than a fixed queue.

Omission is the default, not the exception

English requires an explicit subject in almost every sentence — a bare verb with nothing in front of it usually isn't grammatical on its own. Japanese drops whatever's already understood from context as a matter of course:

にちようびに友達と行きますか。
"[Are you] going with a friend on Sunday?"

Completely normal Japanese with no subject anywhere in sight — the equivalent English sentence needs something there to parse at all. Learners coming from English tend to over-include things reflexively (restating the subject every time, repeating the time when it's already obvious from context) because in English, leaving them out usually breaks the sentence. In Japanese, leaving them out is often the more natural choice.

Particles map onto verb roles; prepositions map onto fixed relationships

This is really what's behind the で/に shift covered in the sentence-structure note. English prepositions (in, at, by, to) map fairly directly onto physical or logical relationships that don't change much. Japanese particles map onto grammatical roles relative to the verb, and which role that is depends entirely on what the verb is doing — so the same particle can translate to different English prepositions depending on context, and particles that look interchangeable in English translation can be doing structurally different jobs underneath.

The synthesis

Taken together, these aren't separate quirks — they're one underlying difference wearing different hats. English builds a sentence around a subject and fills fixed word-order slots around it. Japanese builds a sentence around a topic and a verb, with everything in between tagged by role rather than position, and anything already understood left out by default.

The practical upshot: sentence production stops feeling like translation — finding the Japanese word for each English word, in the same order — the moment it starts feeling like actually building a Japanese sentence: decide the topic, decide what's actually new or worth saying, tag each piece with the role it plays relative to the verb, and let the verb anchor the end.
