Japanese sentence structure

Andrew Brittle, 2026

The "Sentence order" reference in the drill app is built around one specific pattern — the motion sentence from Minna no Nihongo Lesson 5. It's a useful anchor, but it raises an obvious question: how much of this is "how Japanese sentences work" and how much is specific to sentences about going somewhere? The short answer is that most of it generalises — only one slot is genuinely travel-specific — and understanding why it generalises turns out to reveal some of the bigger structural differences between English and Japanese, which is the subject of the companion note linked below.

The motion-sentence frame

[topic]は [time]に [companion]と [means]で [destination]に 行きます / 来ます / 帰ります (か)。

田中さんは土曜日に友達と電車で大阪に行きますか。
Tanaka-san wa doyoubi ni tomodachi to densha de Oosaka ni ikimasu ka.
"Is Tanaka-san going to Osaka by train with a friend on Saturday?"

What generalises, and what doesn't

Four of the six pieces work with almost any verb, not just motion verbs:

  • は (topic) — introduces what the sentence is about, regardless of verb.
  • に (time) — a specific point in time, regardless of verb.
  • と (companion) — "together with," regardless of verb. 友達と食べます ("eat with a friend") is just as normal as 友達と行きます.
  • +か — the question marker, tacked onto the end of any sentence.

The one piece that's genuinely tied to motion verbs is the destination-に slot. に marking "where you're headed" only exists with verbs of actual movement (行く, 来る, 帰る, and a few others). Take that verb away, and that slot gets replaced by something else entirely, depending on what kind of verb is now doing the work.

A plain action verb with an object

A verb like eat, read, write, or buy drops the destination slot and picks up を instead, marking the direct object:

わたしは土曜日に友達とレストランでケーキを食べます。
"I'm eating cake with a friend at a restaurant on Saturday."

で is still there, but its job has quietly changed. In the travel sentence, で marked means — でんしゃで, "by train." Here it marks where the action happens — レストランで, "at the restaurant." で genuinely does both jobs (method/tool, and location-of-action), and the verb determines which meaning applies.

Existence verbs (います / あります)

"There is/are" verbs bring に back, but with yet another meaning — not a destination being travelled to, but a place something already occupies:

公園に猫がいます。
"There's a cat in the park."
A real trap for learners coming from the motion pattern: it looks like the same に, but it's doing a different job — location of existence rather than location of movement. Japanese teaching materials flag this に/で contrast explicitly, precisely because English doesn't distinguish these with different words the way Japanese does.

Verbs like 会う (to meet)

に isn't fundamentally about "destination" at all — it's closer to the target or endpoint of the verb, and travelling toward a place is just one instance of that:

友達に会います。
"[I'm] meeting a friend."

So the honest summary: only the destination slot is travel-specific. は, と, time-に, and +か are genuine, reusable building blocks for Japanese sentences generally.
